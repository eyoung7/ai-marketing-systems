# Skill: Campaign Brief Builder

## Purpose

Turn business context, audience, offer, channel, evidence, and constraints into a structured campaign brief — the kind that lets a creative, media, or content team start work without a round of clarifying questions.

## When to use

At the start of any campaign, before creative or media planning begins. Also useful mid-project when a campaign has drifted and needs to be re-grounded in what it's actually supposed to do.

## Inputs

- The business objective driving the campaign
- What's known about the target audience
- The offer, product, or message being promoted
- Any proof points, data, or evidence available to support the message
- Constraints: budget, timeline, channels available or off-limits, legal/brand guardrails

## Process

1. **State the objective as a business outcome**, not an activity. "Run a LinkedIn campaign" is an activity; "generate qualified pipeline for [segment] before end of quarter" is an objective.
2. **Define the audience specifically enough to write for.** A named segment with a real pain point, not "marketers" or "IT buyers."
3. **Name the problem before the message.** The message should be a response to a specific problem the audience has — if the problem isn't stated, the message is guessing.
4. **Attach proof to the message.** Every claim in the message should point to a proof point from the inputs. If there's no proof for a message angle, flag it rather than writing around the gap.
5. **Note channel-specific considerations**, not a generic message repeated everywhere — what changes about the message or format per channel.
6. **State assumptions explicitly.** Anything the brief relies on that wasn't confirmed in the inputs (audience size, timing, budget availability) gets written down, not baked in silently.
7. **Define how success will be measured** before the campaign launches, not after.

## Rules / guardrails

- Don't invent proof points, data, or customer evidence that wasn't in the inputs.
- Don't write the creative — this produces the brief the creative work starts from, not the ad copy itself.
- If the objective, audience, or proof points are missing or too vague to brief from, say so — don't fill the gap with a plausible-sounding placeholder and move on.

## Output format

```
OBJECTIVE
[business outcome, with a timeframe if known]

AUDIENCE
[specific segment and what's known about them]

PROBLEM
[the specific pain point this campaign responds to]

MESSAGE
[the core message, stated as a claim]

PROOF
[what supports the message — or "none provided" if there isn't any]

CTA
[the specific action the audience should take]

CHANNEL CONSIDERATIONS
- [channel]: [what's different about the message/format here]

ASSUMPTIONS
- [anything the brief relies on that wasn't confirmed]

MEASUREMENT PLAN
[how success will be evaluated, and by when]

REVIEW GATE
[who needs to approve this brief before creative/media work starts]
```

## Human review points

- The objective and measurement plan, before any budget is committed — misaligned objectives are expensive to discover after launch.
- Any message where "PROOF" is empty — a human decides whether to proceed without proof, soften the claim, or hold the brief until evidence exists.
- The full brief, before it's handed to a creative or media team as final.

## Example invocation

> "We're launching [offer] to [segment] next quarter. Budget is [X], primary channel is paid social plus email. Here's the one-pager with the proof points we have. Build the campaign brief."

Expected response: the structured brief above, with any missing inputs (audience specificity, proof, measurement plan) called out rather than quietly assumed.
